# J-1 Filing Path

This note is the local starting point for J-1 exchange visitors.

## Step 1: classify the J-1 role correctly

- `Student`: J-1 status for study at an academic or vocational institution.
- `Teacher or trainee`: J-1 status for teaching, research, training, au pair, physician, camp counselor, or other non-study exchange activity.

This classification matters because the exempt-individual clock is different.

## Step 2: determine tax residency first

For substantial-presence purposes, a J-1 alien may exclude days in the United States only while treated as an exempt individual.

### J-1 student

- Generally can exclude U.S. presence for up to 5 calendar years.
- This is generally a lifetime cap, not a rolling annual count.
- The count is by calendar year, not by anniversary date.

### J-1 teacher or trainee

- Generally can exclude U.S. presence for no more than 2 calendar years out of the last 6.
- The J-1 IRS page notes a limited 4-year exception in some cases.
- Unlike the student rule, the teacher-or-trainee rule can renew after enough time outside the lookback window.

After the exempt-individual period ends, count actual U.S. presence days under the substantial presence test.

## Step 3: choose the annual filing path

### Nonresident J-1 with no taxable income

- File `Form 8843` only.
- This is the common path for someone who was present in J-1 status but had no income reportable on Form 1040-NR.

### Nonresident J-1 with taxable U.S. income

- File `Form 1040-NR`.
- Attach `Form 8843`.
- Collect at least: `W-2`, `1042-S`, `1099`, passport/visa details, `DS-2019`, U.S. entry history, prior-year U.S. presence history, and treaty article details if applicable.

### J-1 who became a resident alien

- Once the substantial presence test is met, the return usually shifts to resident-return rules.
- If the person relies on a treaty tie-breaker and claims residence in the treaty country instead, that is a different path and may require `Form 8833`.

## Common J-1 tax rules to check early

- A nonresident J-1 who performs authorized services for the visa purpose is generally exempt from U.S. Social Security and Medicare tax on those wages.
- Federal income tax withholding can still apply even when FICA does not.
- Treaty benefits are country-specific and article-specific. A treaty country alone does not prove a J-1 exemption.
- Many resident-style 1040 assumptions in the rest of this pack do not apply to a nonresident Form 1040-NR filer.

## Common mistakes

- Using `Form 1040` instead of `Form 1040-NR` while still a nonresident.
- Forgetting `Form 8843`.
- Counting exempt years by arrival anniversary instead of by calendar year.
- Assuming all J-1 holders get the same treaty benefit.
- Using resident-only benefits, especially the standard deduction and common credits, without checking nonresident rules.
