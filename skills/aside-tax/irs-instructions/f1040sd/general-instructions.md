### General Instructions

Use Schedule D to report the following.

* The overall gain or loss from transactions reported on Form 8949.
* Certain transactions you don't have to report on Form 8949 (see Lines 1a and 8a, later).
* Gain from Form 2439 or Form 6252 or Part I of Form 4797.
* Gain or loss from Form 4684, 6781, or 8824.
* Gain or loss from a partnership, S corporation, estate, or trust.
* Capital gain distributions not reported directly on Form 1040 or 1040-SR, line 7 (or effectively connected capital gain distributions not reported directly on Form 1040-NR, line 7).
* Capital loss carryover from prior years.

Complete Form 8949 before you complete lines 1b, 2, 3, 8b, 9, or 10 of Schedule D.

#### Other Forms You May Have To File

**Form 461** to calculate excess business losses.

**Form 8949** to report sales and exchanges of capital assets. You must complete Form 8949 before completing lines 1b, 2, 3, 8b, 9, or 10 of Schedule D. Form 8949 is also used to make gain deferral elections.

**Form 4797** to report:

* Sales of business property
* Depreciable and amortizable business property
* Oil, gas, and mineral property
* Section 126 property
* Involuntary conversions (excluding casualty or theft)
* Noncapital asset dispositions
* Small business investment company stock losses
* Section 1244 stock losses
* Securities held in trading businesses (mark-to-market elections)

**Form 4684** to report involuntary conversions from casualty or theft.

**Form 6781** to report section 1256 contracts and straddles.

**Form 8824** to report like-kind exchanges involving business or investment property.

**Form 8960** to calculate net investment income tax on Schedule D gains.

**Form 8997** to track Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF) investments held during the tax year and to report deferred gains and dispositions.

#### Capital Assets

Most property you own and use for personal purposes or investment is a capital asset. Examples include your home, household furnishings, and stocks or bonds held as investments in your personal account. For more information about what is and is not a capital asset, see Pub. 544 and Pub. 550.

**Non-capital assets include:**

* Inventory or property held primarily for sale to customers in the ordinary course of your trade or business
* Accounts or notes receivable acquired in the ordinary course of a trade or business for services rendered or from the sale of property described above
* Depreciable property used in your trade or business, even if it is fully depreciated
* Real property used in your trade or business
* A copyright; a literary, musical, or artistic composition; a letter or memorandum; or similar property that is your personal creation or was created for you (or given to you in a way that entitled you to the basis of the person who created it)
* A U.S. Government publication (including the Congressional Record) that you received from the government free or for less than the normal sales price, or that you got by way of someone who had received it free or for less than the normal sales price
* Certain commodity derivative financial instruments held by a dealer
* Certain hedging transactions entered into in the normal course of business
* Supplies of a type regularly used or consumed in the ordinary course of your trade or business

> **TIP:** Certain musical compositions and copyrights may qualify for capital asset treatment through election.

#### Basis and Recordkeeping

Basis is the amount of your investment in property for tax purposes. The basis of property you buy is usually its cost. There are special rules for determining basis in certain situations, such as property received as a gift or inheritance.

Keep records that show the purchase price of securities, including commissions and any adjustments to basis such as stock splits, additional share purchases, capital distributions, and depreciation. You need these records to figure your gain or loss when you sell or otherwise dispose of the property.

If you receive a Schedule A (Form 8971) from the executor of an estate, you may need to report basis consistent with the estate tax value. See Publication 551 (Basis of Assets) and Publication 550 (Investment Income and Expenses) for comprehensive guidance on basis.

#### Short-Term or Long-Term

If you hold a capital asset for one year or less, any gain or loss is short-term. If you hold a capital asset for more than one year, any gain or loss is long-term.

Beginning in 2018, certain "applicable partnership interests" (carried interests) require holding periods exceeding three years for gains to be treated as long-term capital gains.
